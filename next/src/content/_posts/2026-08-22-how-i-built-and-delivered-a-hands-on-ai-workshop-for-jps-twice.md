In August 2025, Jamaica Public Service invited me to run an AI content-creation session for their team. In August 2026, they invited me back.

Repeat invitations are the only metric that matters in corporate training. A feedback form can tell you people enjoyed the day; a second booking tells you the first one actually changed how they work. This is the story of what I built, what I learned delivering it, and what I'd tell anyone asked to do the same.

## The problem with most AI training

Most workplace AI sessions fail the same way: a presenter talks at a room about what AI *can* do, shows a few impressive demos, and sends everyone home inspired and helpless. Inspiration fades by Monday. Capability doesn't.

My rule for these sessions is simple: **every participant builds something real before they leave.** No demos of my screen for an hour. Not slides about prompts. Actual hands, actual tools, actual work products.

## The 3E framework

Every session I run is built on three E's — **Entertain, Engage, Educate** — in that order, and deliberately so.

- **Entertain.** If the room is bored in the first ten minutes, nothing after it lands. I open with energy: a story with stakes, a live moment that gets a laugh or a "wait, what?" Attention is the entry fee.
- **Engage.** Entertainment earns attention; engagement converts it into participation. Phones out, laptops open, everyone doing the activity within the first hour — not after lunch, not "once we cover the theory." The six-phase structure exists to guarantee this.
- **Educate.** Only now do the lessons stick, because they're attached to something the participant just *did* rather than something they merely heard. Education delivered last, on top of engagement, outlasts any lecture.

Most corporate training runs the sequence backwards — education first, delivered dryly, with engagement hoped for and entertainment forgotten. The 3E order is why rooms like this one stay switched on for a full day.

## What the workshop covers

I structured the day as a six-phase journey rather than a lecture:

1. **Discover** — a digital presence audit. Before touching any tool, each person sees what the internet currently says about them.
2. **Define** — settle on a core message worth amplifying.
3. **Create** — script and film a short video on a phone. Nothing fancy.
4. **Enhance** — edit in CapCut against a guided checklist: trim pauses, order the story hook-message-proof-close, add captions.
5. **Share** — peer review with two stars and one cut, then publish.
6. **Grow** — turn the output into LinkedIn presence and portfolio habits.

The phase structure matters more than it looks. It converts a vague topic ("AI") into a sequence of small, finishable wins. By lunch, everyone has shot footage. By mid-afternoon, everyone has an edited video. By close, several have published.

## What actually happens in the room

Here's the part no curriculum document captures: the room does not move at one speed.

Some participants arrive already using ChatGPT daily and need pushing on depth. Others have never opened an AI tool and need permission to experiment without breaking anything. The photos from this year's session show what I consider success: every laptop open, every phone out, nobody watching me talk.

I keep three things running throughout:

- **A visible timer per phase** — time pressure is a feature, not a stressor. It forces "good enough to move on" decisions.
- **An interactive app instead of slide decks** — participants follow along in a browser-based guide with checklists, examples and fallback summaries, so nobody has to squint at a projector or fall behind on step four while we're on step seven.
- **Specific, individual feedback** — when reviewing someone's video draft or LinkedIn profile, "nice job" is useless. "Your hook is eight seconds too long and your proof beat buries the number" changes their work immediately.

## What I'd change, honestly

The first year, I over-prepared content and under-prepared pacing. This year I cut two segments entirely and gave the saved time to hands-on editing — the right call. The segments where participants *make* things generate ten times the energy of the segments where I explain things.

Second: captions matter more than polish. Several participants said the automatic-captioning step was the single most useful thing they took back to their daily work, because most people watch video muted.

## What JPS got out of it

A room full of staff who went from "AI is something other people use" to having personally scripted, filmed, edited and shared a video using AI-assisted workflows. That shift — from audience to operator — is the entire point, and it's why the invitation came back a second time.

## If you're planning corporate AI training

Three rules I'd hand to anyone running one of these:

1. **Entertain first, engage second, educate third.** The 3E sequence is the whole game — attention, then participation, then lessons that stick.
2. **Build, don't demo.** If participants haven't produced something by the end, the session failed regardless of the feedback scores.
3. **Structure beats content.** Six clear phases beat forty informative slides.
